Spot the error Q4 Source: Chapter 26 — Prospect Theory

Daniel Kahneman defines loss aversion as the tendency to weigh losses about half as heavily as equivalent gains. What's wrong with this statement?

Your answer

Losses are weighed roughly twice as heavily as gains, not half. The statement inverts the ratio — Kahneman's loss-aversion coefficient is around 2, meaning a $100 loss feels about as bad as a $200 gain feels good.

Agent: correct
The flaw

The ratio is inverted. Kahneman reports a loss-aversion coefficient of approximately 2 — losses are weighed about twice as heavily as equivalent gains, not half.

Why this matters

The asymmetric weighting is what makes prospect theory predict status-quo bias and the endowment effect. Halve the ratio and you lose the explanatory engine — gains and losses would feel close to symmetric, and the theory collapses into expected-utility.
